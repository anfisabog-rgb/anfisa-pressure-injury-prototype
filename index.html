Injuries caused by the devices meant to help

This project started with a person, not a prompt. After a family friend had major surgery, she told me about the long stretches of immobility in the hospital and the pressure injuries that formed where medical devices sat against her skin. I wanted to understand why that happens and whether something simple could catch it earlier.

Medical device-related pressure injuries (MDRPIs) form when equipment such as breathing tubes, oxygen masks, feeding tubes, and catheters presses on the same small area of skin for hours. That sustained pressure squeezes the tiny blood vessels underneath, cutting off oxygen and nutrients to the tissue. Hospitals classify them as "never events," injuries that should not happen at all.

Today, prevention depends mostly on nurses checking skin by eye and repositioning patients on a schedule. Both take staff time that hospitals often don't have, and visual checks are less reliable on darker skin, where early redness is harder to see.

Can a low-cost, multi-point sensor system detect sustained pressure at medical-device contact points in real time, and warn someone before visible damage develops?

What the literature told me to build

Before choosing any parts, I wrote a literature review on how pressure injuries form, who is most at risk, which devices cause them, and why current prevention falls short. Three findings shaped the design:

  • Duration matters as much as force. Pressure above the typical capillary range (about 12 to 32 mmHg) restricts blood flow, but damage depends on how long it lasts. A sensor that reacts to every brief spike would be useless.
  • Head-mounted devices cause the most injuries. Masks, endotracheal tubes, nasal cannulas, and feeding tubes make up the largest share, so the sensors had to be thin enough to sit under straps and tubing.
  • Current prevention is manual and inconsistent. Skin checks, repositioning schedules, and foam dressings show limited or uncertain benefit. The gap is real-time pressure measurement that works without a person watching.

Background

Modern intensive care units (ICUs) are tasked with handling complex processes requiring specialized devices. However, prolonged usage of those devices puts the patient at risk of hospital-acquired pressure injuries (HAPIs), which are injuries caused by prolonged pressure on the patient's skin, cutting off blood circulation and causing tissue damage. These device-related injuries, known as medical device-related pressure injuries (MDRPIs), are different from traditional pressure injuries as they cause significant injury in the location on the skin where the device is in contact. MDRPIs are caused by everyday usage of medical equipment (e.g. endotracheal tubes, urinary catheters, nasogastric tubes and oxygen face masks) and hurt the quality of life for patients by resulting in longer hospital stays and increased patient mortality, leading to their classification as a "never event," or an accident that should never happen in healthcare environments (Roderman et al. 2024). A single severe injury can cost hospitals $20,900–$151,700. Every year, approximately 2.5 million patients in the United States are affected by pressure injuries, which cost U.S. hospitals between $9.1 and $11.6 billion (Zhang et al. 2024). Among them, MDRPIs account for ~30% of all pressure injuries (Öznur 2024). These events also lead to over 17,000 lawsuits against hospitals each year, making MDRPIs a significant "uninsured" issue (Zhang et al. 2024). In this context, "uninsured" does not refer to a lack of patient insurance; it refers to how pressure injuries are not covered by any insurance, highlighting a need for preventative tools, such as smart low-cost pressure sensors, to reduce hospitals' financial burden and mitigate blood circulation loss caused by medical devices. This leads to the research question: "What role can low-cost smart sensors play in mitigating pressure-induced blood circulation drops caused by medical devices?"

Pressure injuries

Pressure injuries (PIs), sometimes referred to as "pressure ulcers" (although not preferred by the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel due to its misleading connotation, as skin ulceration may not be present in early stages), are areas of premature death of living cells or body tissue (i.e. necrosis) resulting from a combination of friction, causing abrasions in the epidermis and superficial dermis; shearing forces occurring as deeper tissues move against the resistance of superficial ones, further disrupting circulation as blood vessels are stretched and torn; moisture, commonly in the form of sweat or incontinence, which softens the skin and makes it more susceptible to damage; and, primarily, pressures that exceed the typical capillary range of 12 to 32 mmHg, which impede oxygenation and compromise blood flow in the affected tissue. PIs often occur over bony prominences, such as the sacrum, heels, hips, coccyx, elbows, shoulder blades, ankles, occiput, knees, and ears, as blood flow is interrupted from the skin and tissues (Mervis & Phillips 2025; Mondragon & Zito 2024). Prolonged tissue compression and mechanical deformation lead to inflammation, cell deformation, and a lack of blood flow, oxygen, and nutrients (i.e. ischemia) as a deep tissue injury forms (Milner & Mathis 2024). Inflammatory responses are triggered as a result of necrosis, further increasing the pressure within the interstitium between cells and blood vessels, perpetuating the cycle of tissue damage. As cells are distorted by direct mechanical stress, necrosis is further exacerbated. Moreover, as ischemia occurs, compressed capillaries block blood flow and deprive tissues of essential oxygen and nutrients while preventing carbon dioxide removal (Velozo et al. 2025).

The National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP) classifies PIs into stages based on tissue damage depth (Mervis & Phillips 2025). Stage 1 serves as an early indicator of PI risk: intact skin exhibits non-blanchable redness, an area that stays red even when gently pressed, indicating internal capillary damage and compromised blood flow. Although ulceration is not yet present, the skin is highly susceptible to it, especially as pressure, shear, friction, and/or moisture is sustained. Stage 2 represents the transition from superficial skin changes to a breach of skin integrity, as the epidermis is penetrated and the injury extends into the dermis. This stage most frequently results from skin and bone moving in opposite directions (i.e. shear) and excessive moisture or heat. The wound features an open ulcer with a pink or red, visibly moist bed, and intact or ruptured serum-filled blisters may appear. The distinction between stage 2 and stage 3 is the visibility of adipose tissue, slough, eschar, or granulation tissue. Stage 3 features full-thickness skin loss, compared with the partial-thickness skin loss of stage 2, with damage extending into fatty tissue. Dead tissue in the form of moist yellow, white, or tan tissue (slough) or thick black or brown tissue (eschar) may be present, along with new, bumpy, red connective tissue. Stage 3 wounds are highly susceptible to infection due to their open and deep nature. If bone, tendon, muscle, fascia, ligament, or cartilage is exposed, the wound is classified as stage 4, the most severe stage, featuring full-thickness skin and tissue loss. This leaves the wound open to serious infection such as osteomyelitis (bone infection) and sepsis, in which the body's overactive response to infection damages its own tissues and organs. When the depth of tissue damage cannot be determined because it is obscured by slough or eschar, the PI is classified as unstageable until the necrotic tissue is removed. Lastly, PIs may be classified as deep tissue pressure injuries (DTPIs) when purple or maroon discoloration is visible and non-blanchable while the skin may be intact. DTPIs originate from intense or prolonged pressure and shear at the bone-muscle interface and work their way up, while stages 1 to 4 start closer to the surface and progress downward. DTPIs also have a hidden phase in which the internal damage is not visible for about 48 hours (NPIAP 2026; Kirman 2024; Mervis & Phillips 2025).

Risk factors

Due to thinning skin, decreased subcutaneous fat, decreased blood flow, and age-related skin conditions most evident in people over 65, susceptibility to PIs increases with age (Mervis & Phillips 2025). Immobilization also increases risk, as patients who cannot shift their weight because of sedation, infirmity, or spinal cord injury experience sustained pressure in one area, leading to ischemia and necrosis. Undernutrition, which prevents the body from maintaining healthy tissue or repairing damage; chronic conditions that impair circulation; and impaired sensation, which disables the body's natural protective feedback, all increase risk substantially.

High-risk medical devices

Certain types of medical devices influence the likelihood of developing PIs more than others. A review of MDRPI incidence among adults in acute hospital settings found that devices attached to the head, such as face masks, endotracheal devices, nasal cannulas, and nasogastric tubing, collectively make up the largest share of devices that cause MDRPIs. This overrepresentation correlates with the high frequency of MDRPIs on parts of the head such as the ears and face (Brophy et al. 2021). Many of these incidents are not regularly reported, so researchers cannot effectively target one type of device until the true scale of the problem is known (Brophy et al. 2021).

Underreporting

Despite a strong push by regulatory bodies (e.g. the U.S. FDA and UK MHRA) for ICU personnel to report PIs, most institutions underreport MDRPIs because they accept voluntary reports instead of mandating them. A British study revealed shortcomings in data collection across institutions, as PIs go underreported and misclassified. The lack of standardized reporting practices prevents accurate comparisons between hospitals' quality of care and the effectiveness of their reporting policies, and many reporting systems lack guidelines for categorizing PIs, contributing to inconsistent data (Crunden et al. 2021). In summary, current reporting policies lack the standardization and accurate classification needed to improve quality of care.

Current preventative measures

Current prevention strategies rely on visual skin assessment and labor-intensive tools for identifying risk. Manual skin checks involve careful examination to detect changes in skin health, but according to Australian researchers who tested this method, assessing long-term changes can take months to years (Borzdynski et al. 2015). Visually assessing early signs such as erythema is even more difficult in patients with darker skin tones, which can lead to inaccurate risk assessments (Borzdynski et al. 2015). Another common strategy is repositioning regimens, schedules of patient positions meant to relieve pressure. A recent review found that repositioning had a limited effect in preventing PIs, with high rates of PI occurrence despite repositioning, though the evidence was limited by sample size and applicability (Gillespie et al. 2020). Researchers also tested prophylactic foam dressings for preventing sacral PIs in a blinded randomized controlled trial; the intervention group showed a slight, statistically non-significant increase in sacral PIs compared with the control group, and cost-effectiveness analysis found the dressings more costly (Walker et al. 2025). All three strategies have limitations that make widespread adoption difficult, especially in institutions facing staff shortages or limited funding.

Research gap

The main gap in PI prevention is the lack of real-time pressure measurement that works autonomously, without constant human supervision. Risk assessment is time-consuming and requires long-term monitoring that most clinicians do not have time for. Manual skin checks and repositioning schedules add workload to a process that could be partly automated by systems that give clinicians feedback even while they are away from the patient. The high cost of some approaches, like prophylactic foam dressings, also deters adoption. These limitations present an opportunity for a low-cost, modular, sensor-based system that automates feedback between device pressure and clinician response. Adjustable smart straps containing pressure sensors could integrate with existing medical device interfaces. If successful, an early warning system like this could improve patient care, reduce costs for hospitals, and simplify clinical workflows.

From breadboard to working system

Each sensor's resistance drops as it is pressed. A voltage divider turns that change into a voltage the microcontroller can read, and the firmware decides what to do with it.

How a reading becomes an alert

  1. Pressure is applied at a device-skin contact point.
  2. The sensor's resistance changes with the applied force.
  3. A voltage divider converts that into an analog voltage.
  4. The Nano ESP32 reads all four channels on A7 to A4.
  5. The firmware finds the hotspot, the highest reading.
  6. The hotspot is classified as safe, warning, or critical.
  7. A timer tracks how long a critical reading lasts.
  8. Outputs respond: LEDs, a buzzer after 10 seconds, and serial logging.

Simple enough to explain line by line

    
The two most important parts are choosing the hotspot and timing the critical state without freezing the program.


int hotspot = max(max(s1, s2), max(s3, s4));

if (hotspot < WARNING_THRESHOLD) {          // below 1200: green
  setOutputs(true, false, false, false);
  criticalTimerRunning = false;
} else if (hotspot < CRITICAL_THRESHOLD) { // 1200 to 2199: yellow
  setOutputs(false, true, false, false);
  criticalTimerRunning = false;
} else {                                  // 2200 and up: red
  digitalWrite(RED_LED, HIGH);
  if (!criticalTimerRunning) {
    criticalTimerRunning = true;
    criticalStartTime = millis();            // start timing, don't block
  }
  if (millis() - criticalStartTime >= CRITICAL_HOLD_MS) {
    digitalWrite(BUZZER, HIGH);              // sustained 10 s: alarm
  }
}

    
Using millis() instead of delay(10000) matters. A 10-second delay would stop the board from reading sensors during that time. With millis(), it keeps sampling every 50 ms, and if pressure drops before 10 seconds, the timer resets.

// Early Pressure-Injury Monitoring Prototype
// Arduino Nano ESP32, four thin-film pressure sensors

const int SENSOR_1 = A7;
const int SENSOR_2 = A6;
const int SENSOR_3 = A5;
const int SENSOR_4 = A4;

const int GREEN_LED  = D5;
const int YELLOW_LED = D6;
const int RED_LED    = D7;
const int BUZZER     = D8;

// Prototype thresholds in RAW ADC COUNTS.
// These are engineering test thresholds, not calibrated mmHg values.
const int WARNING_THRESHOLD  = 1200;
const int CRITICAL_THRESHOLD = 2200;

// Critical pressure must persist before the buzzer activates.
const unsigned long CRITICAL_HOLD_MS = 10000;  // 10 seconds

unsigned long criticalStartTime = 0;
bool criticalTimerRunning = false;

void setOutputs(bool greenOn, bool yellowOn, bool redOn, bool buzzerOn) {
  digitalWrite(GREEN_LED,  greenOn  ? HIGH : LOW);
  digitalWrite(YELLOW_LED, yellowOn ? HIGH : LOW);
  digitalWrite(RED_LED,    redOn    ? HIGH : LOW);
  digitalWrite(BUZZER,     buzzerOn ? HIGH : LOW);
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(GREEN_LED, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(YELLOW_LED, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(RED_LED, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(BUZZER, OUTPUT);
  setOutputs(false, false, false, false);

  // Startup check so each output can be verified.
  digitalWrite(GREEN_LED, HIGH);  delay(200); digitalWrite(GREEN_LED, LOW);
  digitalWrite(YELLOW_LED, HIGH); delay(200); digitalWrite(YELLOW_LED, LOW);
  digitalWrite(RED_LED, HIGH);    delay(200); digitalWrite(RED_LED, LOW);
  digitalWrite(BUZZER, HIGH);     delay(100); digitalWrite(BUZZER, LOW);
}

void loop() {
  int s1 = analogRead(SENSOR_1);
  int s2 = analogRead(SENSOR_2);
  int s3 = analogRead(SENSOR_3);
  int s4 = analogRead(SENSOR_4);

  int hotspot = max(max(s1, s2), max(s3, s4));

  // Serial output for debugging, data logging, and threshold tuning.
  Serial.print("S1: ");  Serial.print(s1);
  Serial.print("  S2: "); Serial.print(s2);
  Serial.print("  S3: "); Serial.print(s3);
  Serial.print("  S4: "); Serial.print(s4);
  Serial.print("  HOTSPOT: "); Serial.println(hotspot);

  if (hotspot < WARNING_THRESHOLD) {
    setOutputs(true, false, false, false);   // SAFE
    criticalTimerRunning = false;
    criticalStartTime = 0;
  }
  else if (hotspot < CRITICAL_THRESHOLD) {
    setOutputs(false, true, false, false);   // WARNING
    criticalTimerRunning = false;
    criticalStartTime = 0;
  }
  else {                                        // CRITICAL
    digitalWrite(GREEN_LED, LOW);
    digitalWrite(YELLOW_LED, LOW);
    digitalWrite(RED_LED, HIGH);

    if (!criticalTimerRunning) {
      criticalTimerRunning = true;
      criticalStartTime = millis();
    }
    if (millis() - criticalStartTime >= CRITICAL_HOLD_MS) {
      digitalWrite(BUZZER, HIGH);
    } else {
      digitalWrite(BUZZER, LOW);
    }
  }

  delay(50);
}

Next validation step: calibration

    
The biggest open question is what a raw reading of 2200 means physically. My plan is to apply known weights over a known contact area (pressure = force ÷ area), record each sensor's output across several trials while loading and unloading, and fit a calibration curve for each sensor. That would let me test repeatability, drift, and hysteresis, and eventually compare the system against a reference pressure-mapping device.

Limitations

    
This is a proof-of-concept, not a medical device. It detects pressure patterns associated with pressure-injury risk. It does not measure blood flow, diagnose ischemia, or predict injuries clinically.

    
    
      
  • Raw values, not mmHg. The 1200 and 2200 thresholds are uncalibrated sensor readings used to test the logic.
    • 
      
  • Sensor behavior. Thin-film force sensors are nonlinear and can drift, show hysteresis, and vary from unit to unit.
    • 
      
  • Pressure isn't everything. Shear, friction, moisture, perfusion, and each patient's anatomy also affect injury risk.
    • 
      
  • Breadboard build. Loose jumpers aren't suitable for a clinical setting. A real product would need medical-grade materials, electrical isolation, and biocompatibility testing.
